## Summary

Crema is the reddish-brown foam on a fresh shot. Physically it's a polyphasic
foam: gas bubbles — mostly carbon dioxide generated during roasting and trapped
in the bean — dispersed in a continuous oil-in-water emulsion carrying dissolved
solids and tiny coffee cell-wall fragments. High pressure dissolves CO₂ into the
brew; when the shot hits the cup and depressurizes, the gas comes out of solution
as micro-bubbles. The foam is stabilized by surfactants: proteins and
melanoidins form it, polysaccharides make it persist, lipids interact with
melanoidins to firm the films. Connoisseurs expect crema to occupy ≥10 % of the
cup and last ≥2 minutes. Crucially, crema indicates **freshness and CO₂ content**
(robusta and fresh roasts foam more) more than it indicates **flavor quality** —
so "the crema is the flavour" is folklore.

## Findings

### 1. What it's made of (C-11)

Illy & Navarini: the gas phase is "mainly the carbon dioxide generated during
coffee roasting and entrapped within the cell structure," while the continuous
phase is "an oil in water (O/W) emulsion of microscopic oil droplets (90 %
< 10 μm) in an aqueous solution of several solutes … containing solid coffee
cell-wall fragments of 2–5 μm." A typical 30 mL arabica espresso is an O/W
emulsion of ~0.2–0.3 % volume fraction, ~150 mg suspended solids (~5 g/L), with
total soluble solids ~52.5 g/L.

### 2. Why espresso foams and drip doesn't

Pressure forces CO₂ into solution during the shot; on release into the cup the
gas nucleates into micro-bubbles. Drip coffee never sees the pressure, so the CO₂
just escapes without forming a stable head.

### 3. What holds it up (C-11b)

"The formation of crema foam is mediated by protein and protein-derived
melanoidins … the persistence of the crema is mainly determined by the extracted
polysaccharides (mainly galactomannans and arabinogalactan) that provide the
viscoelastic interfacial properties," while "lipids interact with melanoidins to
improve the crema stability." Surfactants disrupt surface tension and armor the
bubble walls so they don't coalesce.

### 4. The folklore (C-11c, C-12)

Crema occupies "at least 10 % of the volume of an espresso, and it must survive
at least 2 min before breaking up" — a *visual* quality cue. But it's primarily a
signal of bean freshness and CO₂ load: stale beans have off-gassed and foam less;
robusta (lower oil, different composition) foams more than arabica. So abundant
crema ≠ better-tasting coffee. The article's framing — separate mechanism from
ritual without dismissing either — lands here.

## Terminology
